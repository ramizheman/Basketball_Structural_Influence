\begin{abstract}
\noindent
NBA teams spend billions on players, yet some of the hardest to characterize are the ones who
shape how an offense operates. Box scores barely see them.
Existing analytics measure production, efficiency, and usage, but not how an offense is
organized, or how much its structure changes around any one player.

Teams' offensive organizations are mapped as a network representing players and the play types
they initiate. A player's structural influence is how much the graph's shape changes when he is
removed, meaning how much the offense reorganizes if that player were out. Crossing structural
influence against usage identifies low-usage players whose absence reorganizes the offense
comparable to losing a high-usage playmaker.

Using public data across three NBA seasons, this paper finds that a player's organizational
influence, after accounting for how much he touches the ball, is largely uncorrelated with
value metrics such as net rating. These players, referenced in this paper as shapers, instead
affect the team's offensive footprint.
On the floor, shapers typically mean teams run more cuts and pick-and-rolls and fewer spot-ups
and drives. Their structural influence partly travels with the player after a trade, affecting
a new team's offense too. High-usage organizational talent affects scoring efficiency reliably;
a shaper's structural influence does not. What an offense reorganizes around structurally is
not the same as what makes it score.

With this framework, researchers can study how an offense reorganizes when key players are
unavailable, coaches can prepare for in-game offensive pivots when players go out, and front
offices can see how much a trade or absence changes team identity. Offensive organization
becomes measurable, not just coaching intuition.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\paragraph{A motivating problem.}
NBA teams spend a lot of resources evaluating players, but some of the hardest guys to
evaluate are the ones whose value is mostly organizational. Coaches, teammates, and front
offices talk all the time about ``glue guys'' who connect an offense or hold a system
together, even when their box score numbers look like a rotation player, not a
difference-maker. At the February 2024 trade deadline, Dallas gave up a first-round pick for
Daniel Gafford, a backup center averaging around eleven points and eight rebounds, and made
the Finals with him starting at center. That trade is a clean way to ask the question this
paper is actually about: \emph{what was Dallas buying that production, efficiency, and usage
numbers don't capture?}

There's already plenty of analytics work on how much a player contributes to winning
\citep{rosenbaum2004apm, sill2010winval}, and on what type of player he is
\citep{lutz2012cluster, alagappan2012positions}. None of it really gets at how an offense is
organized, or how dependent that organization is on one specific player. This paper doesn't
prove that glue guys are secretly stars, and despite a lot of testing, it doesn't find that
they add offensive efficiency at the team level either. What it does instead is ask whether
offensive organization can be measured at all, whether some types of players affect that
organization more than others, and whether teams adapt when that organization changes.

\paragraph{Why this is worth measuring.}
Offensive organization is one of the last parts of team performance that's still mostly
qualitative, both for individual players and for teams as a whole. A reproducible measure of
that organization, what this paper calls ``wiring,'' opens up questions existing value metrics
don't touch:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.1em]
 \item how offenses reorganize after an injury or absence, and how well they adapt;
 \item which offenses are structurally \emph{brittle}, leaning on one or two players, versus
 distributed and resilient;
 \item how organizational responsibility shifts as young players develop;
 \item whether a trade preserves a team's offensive identity or rewires it;
 \item which defenders disrupt an opponent's organization the most.
\end{enumerate}
None of these questions prove a player is worth some fixed number of extra points. They're
questions about structure, and depending on what you're asking, they can surface real strengths
or weaknesses in how a team is put together.
As it turns out, that structure is largely independent of scoring efficiency
(Sec.~\ref{sec:efficiency}). What a front office is actually buying, in cases like Gafford,
looks more like organizational continuity than points. Treating organization as something
measurable and testable, instead of something you have to take a coach's word for, opens up a
set of questions basketball analytics hasn't had a way to ask. The contribution of this paper
is the framework itself and the measure it produces. What follows is a first pass at testing
both, focused on one category of player in particular, not a broad survey.

\paragraph{Contribution A: the measurement framework.}
This paper introduces a reproducible, graph-based way to measure offensive organization, and
how it reorganizes when a player is removed. A team's offense is mapped as a graph connecting
players to the play types they initiate. ``Wiring'' is the team-level map of which players
jointly hold which offensive jobs. The test is what happens when a player is removed from that
graph: his \emph{structural influence} is how much the map changes without him. Crossing
structural influence with usage isolates a group this paper calls \textsc{Shapers}: low-usage
players with outsized residual influence on how the offense is organized. This is the
offensive version of the glue guy.

\paragraph{Why a graph, specifically?}
It isn't the easiest or most explainable tool available for this kind of problem, but it's a
natural fit for organizational relationships, which is a different goal than measuring
outcomes. The question here isn't how good an offense is, it's how an offense's organization
changes when a player is taken out of it. A graph lets you remove one node and watch how the
rest of it reorganizes, which is exactly the mechanism this paper needs.

\paragraph{Contribution B: what the framework shows.}
Applying the framework to three NBA seasons (2022--23 through 2024--25) surfaces a few things.
There's a real category of low-usage players who carry as much organizational weight as star
playmakers (Sec.~\ref{sec:emerge}). That influence isn't just value in disguise, it's
statistically separate from net rating and other value measures
(Sec.~\ref{sec:orthogonality}). It shows up on the floor, not just in the math, shifting a
team's play-type mix when these players are in the game (Sec.~\ref{sec:behavior}). It doesn't,
however, translate into more points: having a \textsc{Shaper} alongside a scorer and an
organizer doesn't raise efficiency beyond what talent already explains
(Sec.~\ref{sec:efficiency}). It does still change team identity even when scoring stays flat,
removing a \textsc{Shaper} reorganizes a team's offense about as much as removing a star
organizer does, more than removing anyone else (Sec.~\ref{sec:identity-resilience}). Part of
this influence travels with the player when he changes teams (Sec.~\ref{sec:portability}),
though predicting how well it'll travel in advance doesn't really work, aside from a weak and
unconfirmed signal around cutting versus driving (Sec.~\ref{sec:travelfit}).

\paragraph{Same play-type menu, different wiring.}
Figure~\ref{fig:wiring} shows two teams, Minnesota and Houston in 2023--24, that ran nearly
identical distributions of play types (menu cosine $0.99$) but assigned responsibility for
those play types very differently (wiring cosine $0.54$). Minnesota leans on players who
combine post-ups and spot-ups, or post-ups and pick-and-roll, more of an inside-out offense
built around interior gravity. Houston leans on players who combine cuts, drives, and
spot-ups, a more movement-based attack that uses cutting and driving to create the openings
it then attacks. The team-wide totals for each play type end up nearly the same either way,
because a player who mostly does one thing still adds to that play type's total count, he just
doesn't contribute much to the overlap between play types. Two teams can look identical on a
simple count of what they run and still be organized in completely different ways underneath.
What this framework actually measures is how much that pattern of overlapping responsibility
shifts when a given player is removed. \textsc{Shapers} are the focus of this paper because
their structural influence is large relative to how much they touch the ball or show up in a
box score.

\paragraph{Central claims.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.1em]
 \item Structural influence can be measured from initiation structure alone.
 \item It isn't the same thing as player value.
 \item It travels with a player imperfectly when he changes teams.
 \item Players carry organizational roles distinct from talent that show up in how an offense
 is shaped.
 \item Different players can hold different combinations of responsibility even on teams with
 similar overall play-type distributions.
 \item That influence is partly tied to the player himself, not entirely dictated by team
 context.
 \item \textsc{Shapers} make this otherwise-hidden influence visible.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Positions and archetypes.}
There have been various efforts to re-organize the five positions using box-score statistics
and shot locations into data-driven ``archetypes''
\citep{lutz2012cluster,alagappan2012positions}. These describe what a player does, but not
how that player's actions relate to the actions of other players on the team. They also do
not ask whether that role follows the player when he changes teams. In this paper,
organizational function is defined by relational displacement (Sec.~\ref{sec:framework}),
and player portability is tested by looking at what happens when players move to a new team.

\paragraph{Player similarity and embeddings.}
Player embeddings can be used to find players who are similar based on their data
\citep{sill2010winval}. This is useful for describing similarity between players, but it does
not address whether a player's organizational role carries over to another team.

\paragraph{Lineup and value models.}
Plus/minus models and net rating measure player and lineup performance and identify which
groups perform well \citep{rosenbaum2004apm, sill2010winval}. They are designed to measure
value, not how an offense is organized or whether a player's organizational role can carry
to another team. This paper measures something different. Sec.~\ref{sec:orthogonality} and
Sec.~\ref{sec:efficiency} show that structural influence does not add scoring efficiency
beyond what existing measures of talent already explain.

\paragraph{Network analysis in basketball.}
Graphs have been used to model basketball possessions and calculate flow statistics,
including work on the ``price of anarchy'' \citep{fewell2012basketball, skinner2010anarchy}.
Previous network studies show that graphs can be useful for studying basketball, but this
work focuses on possession-level flow and team efficiency rather than measuring how
individual players affect the structure of an offense.

\paragraph{The gap.}
Existing work can tell us about player types, player similarity, player value, and how the
ball moves through a possession. It does not directly examine an offense as a map of player
responsibilities: which players combine different play types, which players have an outsized
effect on that structure, and whether that effect follows the player when he changes teams.
That is what this paper tests.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Source and unit.}
The data are possession-level records for three NBA regular seasons
(2022--23, 2023--24, 2024--25). The atomic unit is a \emph{possession}, with additional
context like the initiating player, the play type, players on the court, the points scored,
and other contextual fields like period, clock time, and score margin. After restricting to
possessions with a clear initiator and a play type, the full dataset includes approximately
$6.5\times 10^{5}$ possessions across $90$ team-seasons.

\paragraph{Play types.}
Each possession is labeled with one of $K=8$ initiating play types (pick-and-roll, drive,
post-up, spot-up, cut, putback, pull-up, and transition). The play type describes how a
possession is initiated, reflecting the level at which offensive organization operates.
Appendix~H documents how the description-based inferrer derives labels from public NBA Stats
play-by-play fields (actions, descriptions, assist flags, and shot distance/zone), including
foul treatment.

\paragraph{PNR under-detection and calibration.}
The NBA public play-by-play data does not directly identify pick-and-roll (PNR) actions, so
the description-based inferrer detected far fewer PNR possessions than expected. This matters
because PNR is one of the play types used in the association and structural-influence
calculations.
To correct this, the PNR labels were calibrated to bring each team-season into a consistent
PNR range of roughly $18$--$20\%$. The calibration promotes selected pull-up and unclassified
possessions to PNR using player-level information (e.g.\ shot distance), rather than applying
the same adjustment to every player. The calibrated labels, not the underlying proxy, are
used in all association and structural-influence calculations. Applied consistently, the
resulting PNR rates were $18.3$--$20.0\%$ across seasons.

\paragraph{Initiators.}
The \emph{initiator} is the first offensive player credited with a shot, turnover, foul,
or free throw on the possession. For most play types this coincides with the player who
begins the action (the driver on a drive, the shooter on a spot-up, the post player on a
post-up, the cutter on a cut). On an assisted pick-and-roll, however, the initiator is the
player who finishes the possession (the ball-handler on a pull-up or floater, or the roll
man on an assisted rim finish) rather than exclusively the ball-handler who set up the
action. This paper graphs this terminal-actor relation, rather than passing or assist credit,
because it is the act that determines which player sets each possession's structural context.

\paragraph{Seasons, filtering, and pools.}
For role assignment this paper defines a \emph{rotation pool} of players with at least $15$
minutes per game over at least $30$ games in a team-season, ensuring each player's initiation
distribution is estimable. Players outside the pool are still classified but are excluded when
computing distributional thresholds (Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy}). On-court composition analyses
(Section~\ref{sec:behavior}) retain possessions with exactly five identified offensive players
($99.7\%$ of eligible possessions). The shaper-tier subgroup split in
Sec.~\ref{sec:shaper-tiers-main} was motivated by a review of 2025--26 data. After initial
analysis, 2025--26 was identified as lacking full on-court coverage, which was subsequently
corrected (Appendix~G lists that season's shaper roster). The check itself runs on the
pre-registered three-season sample only.

\section{Measuring Offensive Wiring}
\label{sec:framework}

This section builds the framework (Contribution~A): the offensive interaction graph, its
play-type association matrix, and per-player structural influence, calculated as a network
perturbation. From these calculations, role categorizations emerge, and shapers become
visible through outsized displacement relative to usage. Notation is summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:notation}.

\subsection{Nodes, edges, and the initiation matrix}
\label{sec:nodes}

For one team in one season, let $\mathcal{P}=\{1,\dots,n\}$ be the group of rotation players
and let $\mathcal{K}=\{1,\dots,K\}$ be the group of the $K=8$ play types. The offense is
represented as a graph with two kinds of nodes, players and play types, where the only
connections that exist are between a player and a play type, never player-to-player. Each
connection has a weight: how many times that player initiated that play type. Collecting all
of these weights gives an \emph{initiation matrix}
$\Bmat\in\mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}^{n\times K}$, with one row per player and one column per play
type, where each cell $\Bmat[p,k]$ is simply the count of type-$k$ possessions that player
$p$ initiated.
Each play type's column is then converted into a percentage breakdown across players, so
instead of raw counts, each column shows what share of that play type's initiations belongs
to each player, and those shares for a given play type add up to $100\%$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:bnorm}
\tilde{\Bmat}[p,k] \;=\; \frac{\Bmat[p,k]}{\sum_{q\in\mathcal{P}}\Bmat[q,k]},
\qquad \sum_{p}\tilde{\Bmat}[p,k]=1 \;\;\forall k .
\end{equation}

\subsection{The association matrix (min-overlap co-initiation)}
\label{sec:assoc}

This measure isn't about how often each play type happens on its own, that's a simple count
anyone could pull from a possession table. What matters is whether the same players are
behind two different play types, or whether separate specialists handle each one, since a
possession table alone can't tell you that.

The distinction matters because of what initiation represents: whoever initiates a play type
is the player the offense currently depends on to produce that action. When two play types are
held by the same players, high overlap, those actions function as a linked system. The offense
can move between them without changing personnel, and a defender guarding that player has to
respect both threats at once, since either could come from him. When two play types are held
by entirely separate specialists, low overlap, the offense has divided those responsibilities
into distinct jobs. No single defender has to guard for both, because no single player
represents both threats.
Across all $28$ pairs, this pattern shows how responsibility is organized across a roster:
which actions are combined in the same players, harder to defend and harder to remove without
consequence, and which are divided across specialists, more easily replaceable. That's what
this paper means by an offense's \emph{wiring}. It's also why removing a player and watching
which pairs collapse reveals something a raw initiation count can't: not just what a player
does, but which combinations of team responsibility existed only because he personally held
both halves. See Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-pair-glossary} for what the play-type pairs
represent for basketball strategy and tactics.

Why certain pairs cluster in the same player is itself a basketball mechanism, not an
arbitrary artifact of the data. Some play types share an underlying defensive exploit. A drive
collapses help defenders inward, which is exactly what creates space for a pull-up once the
defender recovers, or a spot-up once a helper strays too far from his own man. Cutting works
the same way from the off-ball side: pulling defensive attention toward the ball opens driving
and cutting lanes elsewhere. A player who is genuinely live in both halves of a pair like this
is attacking the same defensive vulnerability two different ways, and a defender guarding him
has to respect both threats at once. That's what makes certain combinations, drive and
pull-up, cut and drive, show up as real, above-chance links in the association matrix, rather
than arbitrary pairs like putback and pull-up. See
Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-pair-glossary} for a full accounting of what each pair represents
on the floor.

The overlap between two play types is the sum, across players, of the smaller of that player's
two initiation counts (computed on the \emph{raw} initiation counts $\Bmat$, not the
column-normalized $\tilde{\Bmat}$):
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:overlap}
O[i,j] \;=\; \sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}} \min\!\big(\Bmat[p,i],\,\Bmat[p,j]\big),
\qquad i,j\in\mathcal{K}.
\end{equation}
The minimum matters because a player only contributes strongly to the link if he does
meaningful volume in both play types. A player with $100$ cuts and $5$ spot-ups contributes
only $5$. A player with $60$ cuts and $55$ spot-ups contributes $55$. The measure therefore
rewards players who genuinely hold two jobs together rather than specialists who dominate only
one. This is not a sequence measure within a possession. It asks whether the same players show
up doing both jobs across the season.

That team-level number still has a problem: a star player who initiates a lot of every play
type, a Nikola Joki\'c or LeBron James, will show up in a lot of these overlap totals just
because he touches the ball more, not because any two of his play types are meaningfully
linked. To correct for that, each pair's raw overlap number gets compared against a random
baseline built from shuffled versions of the same team.
To separate real overlap from what would occur simply because some players initiate more
possessions than others, each team's initiation matrix is compared with $1{,}000$ randomized
versions. The randomization preserves every player's total initiation volume and each play
type's total team frequency, while randomly reallocating play-type labels across the team. The
observed overlap for each play-type pair is then standardized against its own shuffled
distribution. That standardized distance, rather than the raw overlap count, is what is
recorded for each pair. With $\mathbb{E}_0[\cdot]$ and $\mathrm{sd}_0[\cdot]$ the null mean
and standard deviation,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:assoc}
\Amat[i,j] \;=\; \frac{O[i,j]-\mathbb{E}_0\big[O[i,j]\big]}{\mathrm{sd}_0\big[O[i,j]\big]} .
\end{equation}
Those $28$ standardized values together are what this paper calls a team's \emph{structural
fingerprint}, a team-level object throughout. Players only enter the calculation as building
blocks summed into $O[i,j]$, never as a separate fingerprint of their own. A team looks more
like itself, comparing one half of its season to the other, than it looks like any other team,
and that stays true even after removing how often each play type is simply run. That's the
basis for treating this fingerprint as a real, stable property of a team's offense, not just a
snapshot of one season's noise.

\subsection{Structural influence: how much the offense's wiring changes when a player is removed}
\label{sec:load}

This paper defines a player's structural influence by asking a simple question: what happens
to a team's fingerprint if he's removed entirely?
Take the full fingerprint for a team, built from every rotation player as described above,
and call it $\Afull$. Now delete one player completely, remove every one of his initiations
from $\Bmat$, redo all of the shuffle and calculation processes for each play-type pair, and
get a new fingerprint, $\Aminus{p}$. Comparing those two fingerprints, before and after, is
the idea.
The difference between the full and leave-one-out fingerprints is measured with cosine
similarity, which compares their pattern rather than their overall magnitude. Structural
influence, $\load(p)$, is defined as:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:load}
\load(p) \;=\; 1-\cossim\!\big(\operatorname{vec}(\Afull),\,\operatorname{vec}(\Aminus{p})\big)
\;=\; 1-\frac{\langle \operatorname{vec}(\Afull),\,\operatorname{vec}(\Aminus{p})\rangle}
{\lVert\operatorname{vec}(\Afull)\rVert\,\lVert\operatorname{vec}(\Aminus{p})\rVert}.
\end{equation}
A player therefore has high structural influence when removing him changes the pattern of
play-type relationships, not simply when removing him reduces the number of possessions. A
high-volume player whose absence shrinks the same pattern should not automatically receive
high structural influence.

Figure~\ref{fig:loo-displacement} shows this using two Minnesota players from the same
2023--24 team, before any further adjustment of the measure. The heatmap shows the full,
cell-by-cell change in the fingerprint, not just the single summary number $\load(p)$. This
color differentiation is defined later as the displacement signature
(Section~\ref{sec:signature}) but provides context for the following discussion.

For Rudy Gobert (usage $0.09$, $\load=0.60$): removing him from the fingerprint collapses
several cut-related links. CUT--PNR moves, consistent with his own game, rim runs and roll
starts off ball screens, a real dual threat that forces a defense to guard both the roll and
the backdoor cut. CUT--DRIVE, CUT--SPOT\_UP, and CUT--PULL\_UP move even more sharply, and
those aren't Gobert's own combinations, they belong to other Minnesota players.
Karl-Anthony Towns (usage $0.15$, $\load=0.00$), on the same roster: removing him leaves the
fingerprint almost completely unchanged, because other players were already covering the same
combinations he touched.

A simple count shows cutting gets rarer without Gobert, Minnesota's cut rate falls from
$7.5\%$ to $3.1\%$ of possessions. But a count alone can't say whether the cuts that remain
are still paired with a drive, pull-up, or spot-up from the same player, or have become
isolated actions nothing else backs up. That's what the collapsing CUT--DRIVE,
CUT--SPOT\_UP, and CUT--PULL\_UP pairs actually capture: fewer players cutting and driving or
shooting at real volume, meaning fewer two-way threats a defense has to respect. A cutter who
never drives or shoots off that same threat is easy to sag off. Stated as a hypothesis and not
tested here, a defense can start treating a cut as a one-off help rotation instead of a real
coverage responsibility once fewer players are running it as one half of a live threat.

That contrast, deep and structured for Gobert, shallow and sparse for Towns, is what
structural influence is built to capture, something a raw count of cuts can't show. This is a
hypothetical, leave-one-out calculation based on removing a player from the season's data, not
something observed from an actual injury or game.

\subsection{Usage-residual structural influence}
\label{sec:resid}

Structural influence, $\load(p)$, still has one problem: it partly just reflects how much a
player plays. Someone who touches the ball more will mechanically show up in more of these
overlap calculations, even without any real connective role. To separate structural influence
from volume, this paper compares each player's $\load(p)$ against what you would expect
purely from his usage rate, and keeps only the leftover.
This paper fits a curve (allowing for some curvature, not just a straight line) predicting
$\load(p)$ from usage across every classified player. Whatever a player's actual $\load(p)$
is above or below what that curve predicts, given his usage, is called his residual
influence, $\resid(p)$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:resid}
\load(p) \;=\; \beta_0+\beta_1 u_p+\beta_2 u_p^{2}+\resid(p),
\qquad
\resid(p) \;=\; \load(p)-\big(\hat\beta_0+\hat\beta_1 u_p+\hat\beta_2 u_p^{2}\big).
\end{equation}
A positive $\resid(p)$ means a player's structural influence is higher than his usage alone
would explain. A negative $\resid(p)$ means it is about what you would expect, or lower.
One distinction worth being precise about: the usage used in this residual calculation is a
graph-native measure, a player's share of his team's total initiations, calculated directly
from the same data as everything else in this paper. That is different from the standard
box-score usage rate reported elsewhere (like Table~\ref{tab:ortho}) and used later to build
the taxonomy below. The two usually track each other closely, but they are not the same
number, and keeping them separate means the residual is not just mechanically guaranteed to
look clean against the box-score version.

\subsection{Role categorizations: making shapers visible}
\label{sec:taxonomy}

With both usage and residual influence, players can be categorized into four groups by
crossing the two values. The first cut is usage: is a player above or below the median usage
of the rotation pool, meaning does he play a high-volume or low-volume role. The second cut is
residual influence: is $\resid(p)$ positive or not, meaning does he carry more structural
influence than his usage alone would predict.
Four groups fall out of that:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:taxonomy}
\text{role}(p)=
\begin{cases}
\textsc{Primary Organizer} & u_p\ge u_{\text{med}} \ \wedge\ \resid(p)> 0,\\[2pt]
\textsc{Shaper} & u_p< u_{\text{med}} \ \wedge\ \resid(p)> 0,\\[2pt]
\textsc{Terminal Scorer} & u_p\ge u_{\text{med}} \ \wedge\ \resid(p)\le 0,\\[2pt]
\textsc{Role Occupant / Specialist} & u_p< u_{\text{med}} \ \wedge\ \resid(p)\le 0.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.15em]
 \item \textbf{High usage, $\resid(p)>0$:} \textsc{Primary Organizer}. A high-volume player
 whose structural influence exceeds even what his heavy usage predicts, someone whose
 presence organizes initiation pathways beyond just taking a lot of shots. These are usually
 playmakers.
 \item \textbf{Low usage, $\resid(p)>0$:} \textsc{Shaper}. This is the group this paper is
 built around: players who barely touch the ball but whose absence reorganizes the offense
 more than their usage would suggest.
 \item \textbf{High usage, $\resid(p)\le 0$:} \textsc{Terminal Scorer}. A high-volume player
 whose structural influence is basically just what you would expect from that volume, nothing
 extra.
 \item \textbf{Low usage, $\resid(p)\le 0$:} \textsc{Role Occupant / Specialist}. A low-volume
 player whose structural influence is also about what you would expect, nothing extra.
\end{itemize}
Most players land in the \textsc{Terminal Scorer} and \textsc{Role Occupant / Specialist}
groups. That is expected because most players simply are not unusually structurally important
relative to how much they play, and only a small number clear that bar in either usage
bracket.
Splitting the two high-usage groups apart matters, because it separates the organizers
(players whose usage comes with real organizing responsibility) from simply high-volume
scorers and finishers whose influence is just what their volume already explains. It is
possible for both types to exist on the same roster at once, and this paper covers a real
example of that later (Sec.~\ref{sec:emerge}).
These four categories exist mainly to make \textsc{Shapers} visible as a clear category. The
other three groups are not a separate research focus but could be. In this paper, they are
documented for contrast against \textsc{Shapers}.

\subsection{Displacement signature: measuring the portability of a player}
\label{sec:signature}

Figure~\ref{fig:loo-displacement} already showed a cell-by-cell view for Gobert and Towns,
rather than collapsing each player down to a single $\load(p)$ number. That full picture,
exactly which play-type pairs moved and in which direction when a player is removed, is what
this paper calls the \emph{displacement signature}, $\dA_p$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:signature}
\dA_p \;=\; \operatorname{vec}(\Afull)-\operatorname{vec}(\Aminus{p}) \;\in\;\mathbb{R}^{28}.
\end{equation}
Where $\load(p)$ says how much the fingerprint moved overall, $\dA_p$ says which specific
pairs moved, and which direction. It records exactly which combinations of play types a given
player is holding together.
There is another way to describe a player that might sound similar but is not the same thing:
his own play-type profile, meaning simply what percentage of his own initiations fall into
each play type. That is something you could read directly off a normal stat sheet; it just
describes what a player does. $\dA_p$ is different: it is not about what he does, it is about
what changes on the rest of the team when he is gone. Two players could have very similar
play-type profiles and still have very different displacement signatures, because a profile
describes the player, while the signature describes his structural influence on everyone else.
This distinction matters later, in the portability test (Section~\ref{sec:portability}), which
asks whether a player's own displacement signature carries over to a new team after a trade.
To make sure that any apparent carryover is not just because he plays similarly wherever he
goes, the test compares his signature against decoy players on the new team who share a
similar play-type profile, so any leftover similarity in signature specifically, beyond just
similar play style, can be credited to something more player-specific than just running the
same plays.

\section{Pre-registration}
\label{sec:pipeline}

The portability and efficiency analyses are \emph{pre-registered}. For each analysis, the
methods were fixed before looking at results: a single hypothesis and outcome metric, a null
model, a feasibility check confirming the test could actually be run, and stop conditions
that locked the question and the data in advance, so nothing could be swapped out after
seeing how the results looked. Because players are not independent of each other, players on
the same team or in the same franchise tend to have correlated results, the statistical tests
account for that clustering rather than treating every player-season as separate,
uncorrelated data points. This is what makes each null trustworthy, not just a test that
happened to come back empty because it was not run carefully.

\paragraph{How structural categories differ in offensive initiation.}
The graph does not track assists, within-possession passing chains, screens set without
initiation credit, gravity, or communication. An initiator is as defined in
Sec.~\ref{sec:data}. Category-average initiation play types make the roles more visible.
\textsc{Primary Organizers} start half-court offense on the ball: pick-and-roll
($\approx\!24\%$), drives ($\approx\!19\%$), and post-ups ($\approx\!16\%$) lead.
\textsc{Terminal Scorers} look similar, where drives and pick-and-rolls also dominate, but
they do more spot-up shooting and are less unique overlap across those jobs. Other players
already start the same combinations, so removing the Terminal Scorer mostly just removes
possessions rather than rewiring the offense. \textsc{Shaper} players are typically not
primary handlers. They focus on pick-and-roll ($\approx\!25\%$) and cuts ($\approx\!23\%$).
\textsc{Role Occupants / Specialists} are mostly spot-up shooters ($\approx\!33\%$
spot-ups). High residual influence ($\resid$) means the player's initiation jobs are shared
across play types in a way the rest of the players on the team do not mirror. This is the
structural influence.

\subsection{Is it just another value metric?}
\label{sec:orthogonality}

One worry is that structural influence might not be measuring anything new at all, that it
could just be another way of re-detecting usage, scoring, or how good a player's team already
is. To check this, this paper looks at whether \textsc{Shapers} tend to have unusually strong
on-court net rating, once team quality is accounted for. If \textsc{Shapers} turned out to
have a real, hidden edge in net rating, that would suggest structural influence is secretly
just picking up on player value under a different name. If there's no such edge, that rules
out that explanation, and supports the idea that structural influence is measuring something
distinct from value.

Comparing organizers and shapers as categorized above, before adjusting for team quality,
organizers already beat shapers on raw net rating, $+1.45$ vs.\ $+0.80$. This leaves no
hidden shaper premium to explain. Even after adjusting net rating for team quality,
organizers still lead shapers, $+1.22$ vs.\ $+0.50$, but the gap does not clear a
significance bar ($p=0.10$), so this does not prove the difference is real either.

Raw influence does correlate modestly with net rating, points, and touches, which is
consistent with high-volume players appearing more often in the underlying overlap
calculations. Once usage is removed, those relationships largely disappear. The residual
measure therefore does not appear to be simply re-measuring volume, scoring, or on-court
net rating. In this sample, organizational function and the conventional measures examined
here are capturing different things.

\subsection{Can we actually see what these shapers do on the court?}
\label{sec:behavior}

Having measured who the wiring depends on, and shown that structural influence is not just
another way of measuring player value (Sec.~\ref{sec:orthogonality}), this paper checks
whether the category corresponds to what we actually see on the court: the category-level
version of the rim-running, cut-and-roll interior role shown in the Table~\ref{tab:exemplars}
examples. Using within-team-season comparisons, this paper asks whether a shaper on the court
changes the team's play-type mix. That would corroborate that the category is visible on the
floor, not just in the math.

\paragraph{Shaper presence and play mix.}
Across $73$ team-seasons, a shaper on the floor shifts the mix toward off-ball and
ball-screen actions and away from spot-up and drive actions: cuts $+1.35$\,pp, pick-and-roll
$+0.98$\,pp; spot-ups $-1.25$\,pp, drives $-0.54$\,pp (percentage points of share,
within-team). The category's low usage is what makes this meaningful: shapers are low usage,
so they are not taking many shots. The shift is better explained as reorganization among the
other four players.

For comparison, primary organizers reshape the mix even more sharply (post-ups $+2.33$\,pp,
pick-and-roll $+0.96$\,pp; $58$ team-seasons). The team-season count differs between the two
groups ($73$ vs.\ $58$) because a team-season only enters this comparison if it has at least
one on-court possession with that category present and at least one without. Which
team-seasons clear that bar depends on the category, since organizer and shaper presence are
not totally common across rosters.

These are associations, not causal estimates, for two reasons. First, high-usage categories
mechanically run more of their own play types simply by being on the floor and taking those
shots themselves; some of their mix shift reflects that, not real reorganization. This is
part of why the shaper contrast is more informative: shapers barely touch the ball, so a mix
shift when they are on the floor cannot be explained the same mechanical way. Second, the
minutes when a shaper is off the floor often overlap with bench lineups and garbage time,
situations where the competitive intensity and personnel are already different for reasons
that have nothing to do with the shaper. Comparing a team to itself removes differences in
overall team identity, but it does not remove the fact that ``shaper on'' and ``shaper off''
minutes can come from genuinely different game situations, not just the same lineup with one
player swapped out.

\paragraph{A refinement: rebalancing the offense, not expanding it.}
There are two ways a shaper's presence could change the offense. He could be adding something
genuinely new, more distinct kinds of actions that did not exist without him. Or he could
simply be shifting how often the offense's existing actions get used, without adding anything
new at all.

The data supports the second explanation. Shaper presence does not make the offense's
play-type mix more varied or unlock actions that were not already part of the team's play
mix. Instead, the effect is narrower: with a shaper on the floor, the offense changes how
often it uses actions it already has, leaning more toward cuts and PNR and less toward
spot-ups and drives.

\subsection{Where do we stop?}
\label{sec:efficiency}

The framework so far measures organization, not scoring. A natural next question is whether
it also predicts what matters most in practice: points per possession.

It didn't hold. A measurement of how an offense is organized doesn't have to predict how
well it scores, and this one doesn't. The result puts a useful boundary around the measure:
structural organization is not a hidden efficiency metric.

\paragraph{How this was tested.}
Using every possession where all five players on the floor were classified into a role
($N=4.2\times10^{5}$ possessions across $90$ team-seasons), coverage is defined as an
indicator that at least one organizer, one shaper, and one terminal scorer are all on the
floor at once:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:covered}
\textsc{Covered}_i \;=\; \mathbb{1}\big[\,n^{\text{org}}_i\ge 1 \ \wedge\
n^{\text{shap}}_i\ge 1 \ \wedge\ n^{\text{term}}_i\ge 1\,\big],
\end{equation}
Points per $100$ possessions were then estimated as a function of $\textsc{Covered}$, the
count of each role on the floor, controlled for talent, and fixed for team-season,
opponent, game period, and score margin:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coverage}
\PPP_i = \beta\,\textsc{Covered}_i
 + \textstyle\sum_{c} a_c\, n^{c}_i
 + g\,\text{Talent}_i
 + \phi_{\text{ts}(i)} + \psi_{\text{opp}(i)} + \chi_{\text{per}(i)} + \omega_{\text{sb}(i)}
 + \varepsilon_i .
\end{equation}
Including the category counts alongside $\textsc{Covered}$ matters, because
$\textsc{Covered}$ is a nonlinear threshold built from those same counts: this lets $\beta$
isolate the effect of completeness itself, beyond just having more of each role
individually. The pre-registered hypothesis was $\beta>0$, with a strict significance bar
(team-season cluster-robust $p<0.01$) and a bootstrap confidence interval excluding zero.
\begin{hypothesis}[Coverage $>$ accumulation, T$_{\text{cov}}$]
\label{hyp:cov}
$\beta>0$ with team-season cluster-robust $p<0.01$ and a cluster-bootstrap $95\%$ CI excluding
$0$.
\end{hypothesis}

\paragraph{Result.}
Coverage does not predict scoring efficiency.
$\hat\beta=+0.42$ points per $100$ possessions, small and not statistically different from
zero ($t=0.53$, $p=0.60$), with a confidence interval of $[-1.15,+1.90]$ across $0$. This
held up across several versions of the test (Table~\ref{tab:coverage}): removing the talent
control, adding play-type controls, requiring stricter role classification, the estimate
barely moved. The one term that reliably did predict more points was simply having more
organizers on the floor, $+2.35$ points per $100$ ($t=4.62$), a real effect, but a separate
and unsurprising one: more high-usage playmakers means more scoring. It has nothing to do
with completeness.

The sample gives enough precision to rule out a large scoring bonus from completeness, but
not a small one, the confidence interval's upper bound ($+1.90$) is not quite zero. Because
coverage was designed to be separate from talent, and the estimate stayed small and stable
across every version of the test, this is a limit, not a failed test.

\paragraph{Isolating shaper presence alone.}
A narrower version of this question was also tested: does simply having a shaper on the
floor, regardless of whether the offense is otherwise ``complete,'' change scoring at all?
$\textsc{Covered}$ was replaced with a simple indicator,
$\text{HasShaper}_i=\mathbb{1}[n^{\text{shap}}_i\ge1]$, keeping the linear role counts so
the coefficient reflects shaper presence net of raw accumulation. The answer is the same:
no. $\widehat{\text{HasShaper}}=+0.00$ points per $100$ possessions ($t=0.00$, $p=1.00$)
once everything else is controlled for. A related test, whether having more shapers on the
floor at once helps, comes back marginal ($+1.17$ per $100$, $p\approx0.02$) but does not
clear the pre-registered significance bar, so it is not treated as confirmed. Shapers change
what an offense runs (Sec.~\ref{sec:behavior}), but not how many points it scores doing it.

\paragraph{What this leaves.}
Structural organization is measurable, and it is a real property of how an offense is
wired, but it is not another way of predicting who scores efficiently. That is not a
failure of the measure, it is what the measure actually is: a description of organization,
not a hidden efficiency stat.

As a post-hoc check motivated by an out-of-sample 2025--26 review (Sec.~\ref{sec:data}),
this paper splits \textsc{Shapers} at within-class median residual influence into hub vs.\

\subsubsection{Does the offense's identity still change, even when scoring doesn't?}
\label{sec:identity-resilience}

Structural influence, $\load(p)$, is a hypothetical measure. It answers a question that
never actually happens on a basketball court: if a player's initiations were erased from
the entire season, how different would the offense's fingerprint be? That is a clean
mathematical experiment, but it leaves open a real question: does that hypothetical number
correspond to anything that actually happens in real basketball, or is it a product of
deleting rows from a dataset? If Gobert actually sits out real possessions, does
Minnesota's real, observed offense actually shift the way the hypothetical calculation
predicted it would? This section answers that directly, using real on/off data instead of a
simulated removal. This provides an important check on the leave-one-out measure. If players
with high hypothetical structural influence did not also show larger real on/off changes, it
would raise a serious question about whether the leave-one-out calculation corresponds to
anything observable on the court.

\paragraph{How this differs from the earlier calculation.}
Structural influence, $\load(p)$, was calculated earlier as a hypothetical: delete a
player's stats entirely from the season and rebuild the fingerprint from scratch
(Eq.~\ref{eq:load}). This test uses real data instead. For each player-season with adequate
sample (at least $400$ possessions both on and off the floor), the team's actual
possessions are split by whether that player was on the court, two separate standardized
fingerprints are built, $\Amat_{\mathrm{on}}$ and $\Amat_{\mathrm{off}}$, from those real
subsets, and the difference between them is measured:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:DA}
D_A(p) \;=\; 1-\cossim\!\big(\operatorname{vec}(\Amat_{\mathrm{on}}),\,
\operatorname{vec}(\Amat_{\mathrm{off}})\big).
\end{equation}
Unlike $\load(p)$, which is a hypothetical full-season removal, $D_A$ is built entirely
from real minutes the player actually sat out, real benchings and rest patterns, not a
counterfactual.

\paragraph{Result.}
Shapers show meaningfully more identity change between on-floor and off-floor minutes than
similar low-usage players who are not shapers. $96$ shaper/non-shaper pairs were matched on
nearly identical usage rates (average usage gap of just $0.008$), and shapers showed a
larger shift in $81\%$ of those pairs, with a mean paired difference of
$\Delta D_A=+0.115$ ($t=6.35$, $p\approx 7\times10^{-9}$). The play-type mix itself also
shifted more when a shaper sat, not just the fingerprint
($\Delta D_\pi=+0.011$, $p\approx10^{-4}$).

More directly answering the question this section opened with: the hypothetical leave-one-out
$\load(p)$ correlates strongly with real $D_A$ ($r=+0.60$ overall, $r=+0.79$ within
shapers specifically). That's important because the hypothetical measure and the real on/off
measure are calculated in completely different ways. One asks what happens to the team's
fingerprint if you remove the player from the full-season data. The other looks at what
actually happens when he is on and off the court. They line up.

\paragraph{What this means.}
The leave-one-out framework from Section~\ref{sec:framework} is validated against real
basketball, not just internally consistent math. Separately, even though a shaper's presence
does not move the scoreboard (Sec.~\ref{sec:efficiency}), it does change what the offense
actually looks like while he is on the floor, confirmed here with real, observed data
rather than a hypothetical. The value of a shaper, based on this evidence, is not in
points, it is in helping hold together the team's actual identity and structure. Because
this test was not pre-registered, it is reported as a disclosed follow-up finding, not a
confirmed result, and a clean replication on a new season would be the right next step to
confirm it.

One open question this paper cannot answer: whether any opposing defenses actually detected
and exploited this reorganization in real time. The defensive-attention hypothesis raised
earlier (Sec.~\ref{sec:load}), that a defense can start treating a cut as a one-off rotation
rather than a real threat once fewer players are running it, assumes defenses adjust to the
new shape of the offense. That assumption is untested here. It is possible that opposing
defenses, across these three seasons, did not specifically scout or exploit the exact
reorganization a shaper's absence produces, in which case the offense's structure changed
without any defense actually adjusting to it, which would be consistent with the null PPP
result in Section~\ref{sec:efficiency}. Whether NBA defenses identify and adjust enough to
adapt to this kind of structural reorganization is an open question this paper's data cannot
answer, since it would require studying defensive coverage (which requires other data), not
just offensive initiation patterns. This gap is a natural direction for the defense-side
question raised in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Is organizational function partly player-specific?}
\label{sec:portability}

The portability analysis is a supporting check on whether the measure is real, not the
paper's primary claim. If a player's high structural influence reflects something about the
player himself, not just the old team around him, then his measured footprint should
reappear after he changes teams. This section tests that directly across all movers, then
examines the shaper category as a stress test, since it is the category where low usage
most clearly hides structural dependence.

\paragraph{Design.}
Each mover in this test actually played for two teams. His origin signature captures what
his removal would disrupt on his old team, calculated from real data before the trade. His
destination signature captures what his removal would disrupt on his new team, calculated
from real data after the trade, once he had actually joined it
(Eq.~\ref{eq:signature}). The question is whether those two disruption patterns look
alike: does the same player create a similar structural gap on two completely different
rosters? These two signatures are compared using cosine similarity, called self-similarity
($\text{self\_sim}(p)=\cossim(\dA_p^{\text{orig}},\dA_p^{\text{dest}})$).

The obvious worry is that any apparent match could just be coincidence, maybe both teams
happen to look similar for reasons that have nothing to do with him specifically. That is
what the decoy comparison rules out. For each mover, the test also checks whether his
origin signature predicts the destination team's fingerprint better than a decoy would, a
different player already on the new team whose own play-type profile is closest to his
($K=5$ profile-matched decoys). If a random stylistic double would have predicted the new
team's fingerprint just as well, there is nothing player-specific going on, it is just
``anyone who plays like this would create this pattern here.'' Only when the real mover's
own signature beats his decoy's does that count as evidence that something about him
individually, not just his general style, is carrying over:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:portability}
\Delta(p) \;=\; \text{self\_sim}(p)-\text{decoy\_sim}(p).
\end{equation}
A positive $\Delta$ means the player's own structural footprint predicts his destination
team's footprint better than a teammate who simply runs similar plays would. The
pre-registered hypothesis was that the median $\Delta$ across all movers is greater than
zero, tested with a strict permutation test and bootstrap confidence interval.
\begin{hypothesis}[Portability, T1]
\label{hyp:port}
$\operatorname{median}_p \Delta(p)>0$, with a matched-set (within-pool) exchangeability
permutation $p<0.01$ and a destination-franchise cluster-bootstrap $95\%$ CI excluding $0$.
\end{hypothesis}

\paragraph{Feasibility check.}
$294$ movers were confirmed across $30$ destination franchises. Critically, a player's
displacement signature and his play-type profile are essentially uncorrelated with each
other (Spearman $\rho=0.007$), meaning a positive $\Delta$ cannot be waved away as ``well,
he just runs the same plays everywhere.'' Movers were also carefully ordered by actual
game date rather than season label, since a mid-season trade can leave two different
stints carrying the same season label, which would otherwise risk mismatching stints
across a season boundary.

\paragraph{Result: a player-specific component holds up, on average.}
Across all $294$ movers, median self-similarity was $+0.129$ versus median
decoy-similarity of $+0.049$, an excess of $+0.056$. All three pre-registered criteria
passed. This held up under permutation testing ($p=0.005$) and bootstrap confidence
intervals that exclude zero (destination-franchise cluster-bootstrap $95\%$ CI
$[+0.019,+0.086]$). In plain terms: $58.8\%$ of movers showed positive portability, versus
the roughly $50\%$ one would expect if nothing player-specific carried over at all. A
real, player-associated piece of organizational function does persist across team changes,
but imperfectly, the effect is real but modest, part player, part context. Because players
are not randomly assigned to teams, this cannot be treated as proof of causation, just a
real, robust association.

\paragraph{Differences by category exist, but aren't reliable at this sample size.}
By category (Table~\ref{tab:portability-by-class}), median $\Delta$ was $+0.134$ for
organizers, $+0.124$ for shapers, $+0.083$ for terminal scorers, and $+0.018$ for role
occupants. But the overall test comparing all four groups does not clear significance
(Kruskal--Wallis $H=3.85$, $p=0.28$), so with only $24$ shaper movers, this paper cannot
confidently say organizers travel better than shapers, or shapers travel better than
terminal scorers. These four numbers are reported as descriptive, not as a confirmed
ranking. The rest of this section and Section~\ref{sec:travelfit} stay focused on shapers
specifically, examining that category on its own terms rather than in comparison to the
others.

\paragraph{Shaper portability is a distribution, not one number.}
\label{para:conn-dist}
Among the $24$ shaper movers, three cases anchor the range: Daniel Gafford near the top,
Herbert Jones Jr.\ near the median, and Delon Wright near the bottom
(Fig.~\ref{fig:shaper-dist}; Table~\ref{tab:cases}). These are not career grades, they are
illustrations of what a strong, typical, and weak case of structural portability actually
looks like.

The portability test asks whether a player's removal disrupts the same relationships
across two different teams, not whether he literally reproduces the same raw stat line on a
new roster. A concordance map like Figure~\ref{fig:portability} shows, cell by cell,
whether the origin and destination teams agree on the direction of change for each
play-type pair when the player is removed: consistent weakening colored one way,
consistent strengthening the other, and no agreement left uncolored.

Gafford's map is sparse but deep: two strong, consistent cells on cut-linked pairs dominate
his footprint (self-similarity $+0.86$, $\Delta=+0.60$). In basketball terms, wherever
Gafford goes, he creates the same specific structural gap when he sits, both in Washington
and in Dallas, removing him weakens the same cut-linked pairs, in the same direction, on
two rosters with entirely different personnel. That is a role he genuinely brings with him,
not something his old team happened to be using him for.

Jones Jr.'s map is more scattered, some agreement, some disagreement, a weaker overall
signal (self-similarity $+0.21$, $\Delta=+0.17$). His removal shifts some of the same pairs
in the same direction on both teams, real signal, but it is mixed in with pairs moving in
opposite directions and others with no consistent pattern at all. His role partially
travels, but a meaningful part of what he did structurally looks specific to what his
original team needed from him.

Wright's map shows almost no agreement at all between his two teams (self-similarity
$-0.66$, $\Delta=-0.71$). Whatever structural gap his absence created in Washington does
not match the gap his absence created in Miami. His structural role was not really his own,
it was mostly shaped by what his original team specifically had him doing, and it did not
reappear in a new context.

The lesson: a small number of rock-solid, repeatable structural connections, like
Gafford's cut-linked pairs, matter more than a larger number of loose, inconsistent ones.
A few things a player reliably does everywhere predict his real structural value better
than a broad footprint that only partially holds up once the roster around him changes.
(Gafford's origin window here is his partial pre-trade WAS 2023--24 stint, not the full
WAS 2022--23 season in Table~\ref{tab:exemplars}.)

\paragraph{Does landing on the right team explain the variation?}
The obvious front-office explanation for why portability varies is fit, maybe a player's
role reappears only if his new team has room for it, and fails if someone else already
fills that role. This is tested using a fit score, essentially the inverse of how closely
his new teammates already resemble him structurally:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:fit}
\text{fit\_score}(p) \;=\; -\text{decoy\_sim}(p).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Result: fit doesn't explain it.}
Among shaper movers, fit score barely correlates with portability at all (roughly $-0.09$
to $-0.39$ depending on the measure). Landing on a team that structurally has a hole for a
shaper's role does not reliably predict whether that role actually travels. This is a
precise null on this specific measure of fit, not a claim that scheme fit never matters at
all in any sense.

\subsection{Can organizational function transfer be predicted?}
\label{sec:travelfit}

Section~\ref{sec:portability} established that portability is real but variable, and that
destination fit does not explain the variation. This section asks a narrower question: is
there anything about a shaper's own game, before the trade, that predicts whether his
structural role will travel with him?

The short answer is no, not reliably. A composite score built from $63$ different features
of the origin and destination context was tested, and none of it survives correction for
testing that many things at once (Appendix~C). The single most promising lead is a
player's own cut share before the trade: shapers who ran a lot of cuts on their original
team were more likely to be portable ($75\%$ portable among high-cut shapers, versus
$37.5\%$ among low-cut shapers), but with only $24$ shaper movers, this does not reach
statistical significance ($p=0.13$). It is reported as a directional and unconfirmed finding,
not a prediction tool.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The framework is the main contribution, not any one result.}
This paper starts with a fairly simple idea: NBA teams clearly organize offensive
responsibility in ways that don't show up in ordinary player statistics, but there has not
been a straightforward way to measure that organization. The graph framework provides one way
to do that. The results in Section~\ref{sec:results} are the first test of what the measure
picks up, where it seems to correspond to real basketball, and where it does not.

The first set of results gives the measure some support, but also puts clear limits around it.
Structural influence is not just another value metric: once usage is removed, it has
essentially no relationship with net rating. \textsc{Shapers} also correspond to something
observable on the court, shifting the team's play mix even though they themselves are
low-usage players. And when players change teams, part of their structural footprint follows
them, although imperfectly. At the same time, the framework does not predict an efficiency
advantage. That distinction matters: this appears to be measuring how an offense is
organized, not how good it is.

The efficiency null (Sec.~\ref{sec:efficiency}) is a boundary, not a failed test. A
measurement of organizational structure does not need to reproduce the outcomes used to
evaluate players: structural influence is measurable, but it is not the same as offensive
efficiency. The disclosed on/off identity test (Sec.~\ref{sec:identity-resilience}) then
supplies the follow-up: shaper absence displaces the observed association fingerprint more
than usage-matched role-occupant absence. Scoring efficiency does not move, but identity does.
That is the scoped ``so what'' for the shaper class in this paper: organizational continuity
under substitution, not a PPP effect.

The null efficiency result is still useful because it tells us what structural influence is
not. Losing a shaper changes the observed organization of the offense, but this paper does not
find evidence that the change itself produces a measurable scoring penalty. Organizers are
different in one important respect: having more of them on the floor is associated with higher
scoring efficiency. But that is a separate result and is not evidence that organizational
completeness itself improves efficiency. The simplest interpretation is that organizational
dependence and offensive value are not the same thing.

\paragraph{What becomes measurable now: from players to teams.}
Structural influence identifies who an offense's current wiring depends on. It does not
identify who the offense cannot afford to lose. The framework is not only about individuals.
Structural influence is a team-level distribution, not just a player-level score, and its
concentration across a roster remains a natural candidate for organizational resilience at the
team level. Two offenses can score identically and still differ in how much they depend on one
or two load-bearing players. The player-level on/off identity result
(Sec.~\ref{sec:identity-resilience}) is evidence that this dependence is real under
substitution. Elevating roster influence concentration to a confirmatory team-brittleness
claim is still future work.

With a reproducible measure of offensive wiring, researchers and practitioners can ask
questions that previously had no quantitative handle, at both the player and team level:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.6em,itemsep=0.1em]
 \item Which teams are structurally brittle, and to whose absence specifically?
 \item Which injury would force the largest offensive reorganization?
 \item Which player replacement requires the least rewiring, holding scoring fixed?
 \item Does a given trade preserve or fundamentally rewire offensive identity?
 \item How do coaching systems differ in structural dependence, not only in outcomes?
 \item How do rookies assume organizational responsibility over time?
 \item How does organizational structure evolve across seasons?
 \item Which defenders most disrupt an opponent's wiring (the inverse perturbation
 question flagged below)?
\end{itemize}
These questions can still matter even if structural influence is not a value metric. A team
does not need to know that a player is worth a fixed number of extra points to ask whether
losing him will force a major change in how the offense operates. Identifying where that
dependence exists could be useful for injuries, roster construction, trades, and player
replacement. The results in this paper are a first test of the framework, not a complete list
of what it can eventually be used for.

\paragraph{What remains outside the possession graph.}
Portability is unpredictable given what a possession-initiation graph can see. Other channels
include coaching deployment, spatial fit, defensive scheme, specific on-court partnerships,
and signature noise from low initiation volume. An exploratory panel on ``when to acquire''
(brittleness / multiplier triggers) does not find a timing rule and reinforces the efficiency
boundary (Sec.~\ref{sec:efficiency}).

\paragraph{Future work: the inverse question on defense.}
Everything above is an offensive wiring diagram. The graph also carries defensive on-court
lineups, which opens a question this paper does not answer: which defenders reshape the
opponent's offensive wiring the most. That requires its own construct-validity work before it
can be evaluated with the same discipline applied to portability. Separately, a defense can
already adjust coverage, help, and matchups in anticipation of the known post-removal
play-mix pivot documented in Sec.~\ref{sec:behavior} (more cuts and pick-and-rolls, fewer
spot-ups and drives). That use of the framework does not require a new defender-attribution
model.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
 \item \textbf{Initiation only.} Edges record who initiates each play type, not the full
 pass/screen sequence. Intra-possession chains are outside the data.
 \item \textbf{No player tracking.} No spatial data. Organizational function is inferred from
 initiation alone, which helps reproducibility but limits what can be said about mechanism. As
 an illustration: Draymond Green, commonly cited as an archetypal offensive shaper,
 categorizes as \textsc{Role Occupant / Specialist} under Eq.~\eqref{eq:taxonomy} in all three
 sample seasons, since his organizational contribution is understood to operate primarily
 through passing and communication, both outside what this graph represents.
 \item \textbf{Association only.} Rosters are chosen, not randomized. Portability reflects
 that wiring ability tends to reappear, and origin-side selection is not fully excluded.
 \item \textbf{Small-influence estimation noise.} Near $\resid=0$, taxonomy boundaries are
 approximate for very low-initiation players.
 \item \textbf{Efficiency as a boundary.} The coverage null (Sec.~\ref{sec:efficiency}) marks
 a boundary of the instrument, not a failure of it.
 \item \textbf{On/off identity test is post-hoc.} Sec.~\ref{sec:identity-resilience} was not
 pre-registered. It is a follow-up to the efficiency boundary using a different outcome
 ($D_A$, observed on/off association displacement) from leave-one-out $\load(p)$. A
 prospective replication remains the gold-standard check.
 \item \textbf{$\load(p)$ and $D_A$ use slightly different possession universes.} Primary
 $\load(p)$ is computed on the full initiation-edge universe. Primary $D_A$ uses the on-court
 five-player filtered pool (Sec.~\ref{sec:identity-resilience}). A sensitivity check aligning
 both on the initiation-edge universe yields $r=+0.51$ ($n=908$;
 \texttt{dA\_L\_common\_universe\_check.py},
 \texttt{output/dA\_L\_common\_universe\_check.csv}).
 \item \textbf{Backward-looking by construction.} Origin-side screens extrapolate from what a
 player has been, not developmental upside.
 \item \textbf{Cut/drive screen is exploratory.} Directional at $n=24$ shaper movers, not part
 of the pre-registered portability protocol (Sec.~\ref{sec:travelfit}). A prospective blind
 battery on a new season remains the gold-standard confirmation. 2025--26 was deliberately
 excluded from confirmatory tests.
 \item \textbf{Large residual outside the initiation graph.} Most within-shaper $\Delta$
 variation is unexplained after cut/drive. Destination context is FDR-null.
 \item \textbf{Fit operationalization.} Destination fit is $-\text{decoy\_sim}$
 (Eq.~\ref{eq:fit}), which captures structural vacancy rather than scheme fit or coaching
 trust.
 \item \textbf{Sample and era.} Three seasons, on-court analyses restricted to the tracking
 subset. Median-split thresholds may shift across eras (appendix).
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Most basketball analytics measure what players produce. This paper introduces a reproducible
graph framework for measuring how they organize production. Offenses are wiring diagrams,
team-level maps of who initiates which play types and whether the same players jointly hold
multiple jobs. Structural influence quantifies each player's contribution to that organization
via network perturbation.

The main contribution is not a new value metric for identifying hidden stars. It is a way to
quantify one part of offensive organization that has mostly been discussed qualitatively. The
first application here suggests that this structure is distinct from conventional value
measures, visible in real changes to offensive organization, and partly portable across teams.
The framework also leaves a number of questions open, particularly around team brittleness,
injuries, roster replacement, and defensive disruption.

\paragraph{H. Play-type inferrer from public play-by-play.}
\label{sec:appendix-inferrer}
No Synergy, Second Spectrum, or tracking feed is used. Possession labels are inferred from
NBA Stats play-by-play V3 fields available via the public API, principally
\texttt{actionType}/\texttt{subType}, event description text, assist tokens on made shots,
shot distance, and court coordinates, after events are normalized into a possession chain.
A rule-based classifier maps those signals onto nine labels
(\texttt{TRANSITION}, \texttt{PNR}, \texttt{DRIVE}, \texttt{POST\_UP}, \texttt{SPOT\_UP},
\texttt{CUT}, \texttt{PUTBACK}, \texttt{PULL\_UP}, \texttt{OTHER}); association and
structural-influence calculations use the eight initiating types listed in
Sec.~\ref{sec:data}, with residual \texttt{OTHER} possessions excluded from the canonical
set after labeling (and after the foul- and PNR-calibration passes below).

Rules fire in fixed priority (highest first). Table~\ref{tab:inferrer-signals} summarizes the
main PBP$\to$label mappings; confidence scores accompany each rule so downstream gates can
prefer high-signal labels when needed.
\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{\textbf{Possession classifier signals.} Public PBP signals to initiating play type.
PNR rows use assist flags that appear only on made shots unless noted.}
\label{tab:inferrer-signals}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.52\linewidth}p{0.40\linewidth}@{}}
\toprule
PBP signal (\texttt{subType} / text / assist) & Label \\
\midrule
Putback / tip subtypes & \texttt{PUTBACK} \\
Cutting / alley-oop subtypes & \texttt{CUT} \\
Fadeaway, hook, turnaround (or post text) & \texttt{POST\_UP} \\

\noindent
The strings ``pick'' and ``screen'' never appear in V3 descriptions, so PNR is never read
off a ball-screen token. It is inferred from assist-plus-subtype/distance rules on makes,
a low-confidence missed-pull-up correction, and the player-level calibration pass summarized
in Sec.~\ref{sec:data}. Without calibration, the description-based inferrer alone recovers
PNR initiations at roughly a quarter of their true rate (raw inferrer output
$\approx\!4\%$ of possessions vs.\ an expected $\approx\!20$--$25\%$ from play-type tracking
proxies). The shot-event proxy is used only to set season-level promotion \emph{targets}; it
does not enter the association matrix or structural influence directly. Those objects use the
final calibrated possession labels (inferrer-native PNR plus promoted \texttt{PULL\_UP}/
\texttt{OTHER} possessions). Promotion targets come from a season-level PNR-rate proxy built
from NBA play-by-play shot events (shot subtype, distance, and assist flags on makes), scaled
by a fixed factor of $2.5$ from shot-level to possession-level rates and capped at $33\%$ of
a team's half-court possessions. Each candidate's promotion score is that player's own
historical inferrer-only PNR rate (team rate if under $10$ tagged possessions) times a fixed
prior by current label and confidence
(\texttt{PULL\_UP}: $0.55$/$0.35$/$0.15$ for conf $<0.45$/$0.45$--$0.65$/$\!>\!0.65$;
\texttt{OTHER} at conf $0.30$: $0.25$), so promotion is player-level rather than a flat
team-wide adjustment. A further per-game cap limits drain of any one team's \texttt{PULL\_UP}
bucket to at most half of that bucket; remaining promotions come from \texttt{OTHER}, and the
pass stops rather than overshoot if both are exhausted. Every promoted possession retains its
pre-calibration label and confidence (enabling exact rollback) and is stamped at confidence
$0.22$, below any inferrer-native PNR label. Applied this way across seasons 2022--23 through
2025--26 (the fourth season only for exploratory addenda), seasons land within a point of each
other on PNR share ($18.3$--$20.0\%$).

\paragraph*{Foul treatment.}
Possessions that end in free throws without a classified shot action often fall through to
\texttt{OTHER} at low confidence. A foul-tendency pass then relabels those
\texttt{OTHER}+foul-ending possessions using the fouled initiator's season primary action
type (drive, post-up, PNR, cut, spot-up, pull-up, or transition), stamped at mid confidence
and marked \texttt{foul\_tendency\_relabeled}. Free-throw-only trips that are not attributable
to a half-court initiation are excluded from half-court efficiency tallies where PPP is
computed. A backward attribution audit recovered $98.6\%$ of remaining foul-ending
\texttt{OTHER} possessions by linking them to the initiating offensive action; correcting
misclassified foul endings shifted play-type PPP by at most $0.007$ points and produced no
play-type ranking changes, so the association-matrix corpus inherits the same labels without
a material sensitivity to that residual.


\paragraph{I. Play-type pair glossary.}
\label{sec:appendix-pair-glossary}
Grouped by family, each with the on-court meaning and what a defense loses if that pairing
disappears. Pairs with weak or no natural basketball reading are marked as such rather than
forced into an explanation.

\paragraph*{Ball-screen family.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
 \item \textbf{PNR--DRIVE:} a ball handler who both runs pick-and-roll and drives without a
 screen. High overlap means he is live in ball-screen and isolation actions alike: a
 defender cannot relax off ball-screen coverage expecting he only operates off a pick.
 \item \textbf{PNR--POST\_UP:} a big who both rolls/pops off ball screens and posts up. High
 overlap signals an interior hub who can be fed straight or used in an action, forcing bigger
 decisions from help defenders.
 \item \textbf{PNR--SPOT\_UP:} a player who runs pick-and-roll and also spaces off it. Common
 for a secondary ball handler who initiates the screen action then relocates to shoot.
 \item \textbf{PNR--CUT:} covered in the main text (Gobert's own dual threat: rim runs and
 roll starts).
 \item \textbf{PNR--PUTBACK:} weak natural reading. Likely reflects a big who is live in ball
 screens and also crashes the offensive glass, not a designed two-way action.
 \item \textbf{PNR--PULL\_UP:} a ball handler who runs pick-and-roll and also creates his own
 pull-up jumper off the dribble without one. High overlap describes a primary shot creator,
 hard to funnel into one action.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Drive-adjacent.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
 \item \textbf{DRIVE--POST\_UP:} a player who both attacks off the dribble and posts up,
 usually a wing or forward who can operate from the perimeter or the block. Removes a
 defender's ability to guard him with one strategy.
 \item \textbf{DRIVE--SPOT\_UP:} driver who is also live standing still. Sag off the drive
 and he shoots; crowd the catch and he drives.
 \item \textbf{DRIVE--CUT:} covered in the main text: driver who is also live cutting off the
 ball when he is not initiating.
 \item \textbf{DRIVE--PUTBACK:} weak natural reading, mostly reflects a player active around
 the rim generally rather than one designed action.
 \item \textbf{DRIVE--PULL\_UP:} a player live both attacking downhill and pulling up
 mid-drive. Standard shot-creator combination, hard to defend with a single coverage.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Post-adjacent.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
 \item \textbf{POST\_UP--SPOT\_UP:} the inside-out combination described in the MIN/HOU
 comparison, a player who posts up and also spaces to shoot. Forces a defense to choose
 between fronting the post and closing out on the catch.
 \item \textbf{POST\_UP--CUT:} a big who posts up and also cuts when not posting. Common
 combination for a traditional low-post player who also moves without the ball.
 \item \textbf{POST\_UP--PUTBACK:} natural pairing for a true post player who also crashes
 the glass off his own or others' misses.
 \item \textbf{POST\_UP--PULL\_UP:} weak natural reading. Rare for the same player to both
 post up and create a perimeter pull-up at real volume.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Cut-adjacent (beyond those above).}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
 \item \textbf{CUT--SPOT\_UP:} covered in the main text: cut-and-replace action (cut to the
 rim, relocate if it is not there).
 \item \textbf{CUT--PUTBACK:} a player live around the basket generally, cutting and crashing
 for offensive boards. Common for a low-usage big.
 \item \textbf{CUT--PULL\_UP:} covered in the main text: an unusual combination since cutters
 and pull-up shooters are typically different player types; when it appears, it signals a
 versatile wing.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Spot-up-adjacent (beyond those above).}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
 \item \textbf{SPOT\_UP--PUTBACK:} weak natural reading, likely reflects a floor-spacing big
 who also crashes the glass, not a linked action.
 \item \textbf{SPOT\_UP--PULL\_UP:} a player live catching and shooting and also creating his
 own look off the dribble. Standard high-value shooter combination.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Transition-adjacent.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
 \item \textbf{TRANSITION--PNR}, \textbf{TRANSITION--DRIVE}, \textbf{TRANSITION--POST\_UP},
 \textbf{TRANSITION--SPOT\_UP}, \textbf{TRANSITION--CUT}, \textbf{TRANSITION--PUTBACK},
 \textbf{TRANSITION--PULL\_UP:} transition largely reflects who is simply first up the floor
 or gets the ball in early offense, and tends to correlate with general offensive involvement
 rather than a specific tactical linkage. These pairs are included for completeness but
 generally carry weaker basketball meaning than half-court combinations; treat elevated
 values here cautiously and check whether they are being driven by a player's overall usage
 rather than a genuine two-way half-court threat.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph*{Putback-adjacent (beyond those above).}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=0.2em]
 \item \textbf{PUTBACK--PULL\_UP:} weak natural reading, an unusual combination; a high joint
 value here is more likely noise or a small-sample artifact than a real linked action.
\end{itemize}
